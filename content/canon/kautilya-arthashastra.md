# ARTHASHASTRA

## Book I: Concerning Discipline and the Duties of the King

OM. Salutation to Sukra and Brihaspati. This Arthashastra is made as a compendium of almost all the Arthashastras, which, in view of acquiring and maintaining the earth, have been composed by ancient teachers.

Anvikshiki (Philosophy), Trayi (the triple Vedas), Varta (Economics: Agriculture, Cattle-breeding and Trade), and Dandaniti (the Science of Government) are the four sciences. Anvikshiki comprises the systems of Sankhya, Yoga, and Lokayata. By keeping the mind steady in weal and woe, and by bestowing the power of knowledge, action, and reasoning, philosophy is ever the lamp to all branches of knowledge, the means to all operations, and the basis of all duties.

Control of the senses, which is the root of sciences, is to be acquired by abandoning lust, anger, greed, vanity, haughtiness, and joy. He who has no control over his senses shall, though possessing the whole earth bounded by the four seas, of a certainty perish.

Having acquired mastery over the organs of sense, the king shall associate with elders in wisdom. For from hearing ensues knowledge; from knowledge application; from application proficiency; and from proficiency arises self-mastery.

The king shall divide both the day and the night into eight nalikas (periods). During the first-eighth of the day, he shall post guards and attend to the accounts of receipts and expenditure; during the second, he shall look into the affairs of both citizens and country-people; during the third, he shall take his bath, dinner, and attend to study; during the fourth, he shall receive revenue in gold and superintend the appointment of officers; during the fifth, he shall consult with his ministers in council and receive secret intelligence; during the sixth, he shall take rest or attend to his personal amusement; during the seventh, he shall inspect his elephants, horses, chariots, and infantry; and during the eighth, he shall deliberate on military plans with his commander-in-chief.

In the happiness of his subjects lies his happiness; in their welfare his welfare; whatever pleases himself he shall not consider as good, but whatever pleases his subjects he shall consider as good. Hence the king shall ever be active and discharge his duties; the root of wealth is activity, and of evil its reverse. Through want of activity and diligence, even what is acquired will be lost, and what is not acquired cannot be secured.

All urgent affairs the king shall hear at once, and never put off; for when postponed, an affair becomes difficult or impossible to accomplish. When in his council chamber, he shall not cause his petitioners to wait at the door; for when a king makes himself inaccessible, those around him cause confusion, whereby public disaffection or danger from enemies is engendered.

Sovereignty is possible only with assistance. A single wheel can never move a carriage. Hence the king shall employ ministers and hear their opinion.

## Book II: The Duties of Government Superintendents and the Treasury

All undertakings depend upon finance. Hence, foremost attention shall be paid to the treasury. Public revenue shall be collected like the bee gathers honey from flowers without harming them, and never through oppressive taxation that leaves the cultivator impoverished. An empty treasury devours the king himself.

Just as it is impossible not to taste the honey or the poison that finds itself at the tip of the tongue, so it is impossible for a government servant not to eat up, at least, a bit of the king's revenue. Just as with fish swimming in water it cannot be known whether they are drinking water or not, so with government servants employed in the government offices it cannot be known whether they are appropriating money or not.

There are forty ways of embezzlement: what is realized earlier is entered later on; what is realized later is entered earlier; what ought to be realized is not realized; what is hard to realize is shown as realized; what is collected is made away with; what is not collected is entered as collected; and what is paid in one coin is credited in another.

To curb this, the king shall institute inspections by honest superintendents, keep spies over the collectors, and reward informants who detect fraud. The state should maintain complete accounts of the state mines, forests, toll-houses, public roads, and foreign merchandise, ensuring that the wealth of the realm increases without hardship to the producer.

## Book VI: The Source of Sovereign States (The Saptanga Theory)

The state consists of seven limbs (Prakritis):
1. The King (Swami)
2. The Minister (Amatya)
3. The Country/Territory and Population (Janapada)
4. The Fortified City (Durga)
5. The Treasury (Kosha)
6. The Army/Force (Danda)
7. The Ally (Mitra)

The King, endowed with noble qualities, is the leader and the unifying soul of these limbs. When the king is active, the servants become active after him. If he is slack, they slacken after him, and consume his works.

The country should be free from swamps and hostile factions, furnished with rich agricultural soil, mines, and forests, having loyal inhabitants, and accessible to trade. The fortress should be defensible against siege; the treasury full of gold and gems lawfully collected; the army obedient, brave, and well-equipped; and the ally hereditary, loyal, and capable of rendering timely assistance.

When all seven limbs are sound and functioning in harmony, even a small kingdom can withstand a powerful aggressor. But when the king lacks discipline or the ministers lack integrity, even an empire with vast territory collapses from within like dry wood infested with termites.

## Book VII: The Six-Fold Foreign Policy (Shadgunya & Mandala Theory)

The circle of sovereign states (Rajamandala) consists of twelve primary actors: the King aspiring to conquest (Vijigishu), his immediate neighbor who is a natural enemy (Ari), the friend of the Vijigishu (Mitra), the friend's friend (Mitra-mitra), and so on, together with the intermediate king (Madhyama) and the neutral sovereign (Udasina).

The six-fold policy (Shadgunya) to be applied according to circumstance consists of:
1. Peace (Sandhi): entering into a treaty with agreement on conditions.
2. War (Vigraha): doing injury to the hostile power.
3. Neutrality/Watching (Asana): remaining quiet and observing.
4. Marching/Mobilization (Yana): advancing on a military expedition.
5. Seeking Alliance/Protection (Samshraya): surrendering oneself to or taking shelter with a stronger power.
6. Double Policy (Dvaidhibhava): making peace with one king and waging war with another.

Whoever is inferior to another in power shall make peace with him; whoever is superior in power shall wage war against him; whoever thinks 'neither can the enemy ruin me nor can I ruin him' shall observe neutrality; whoever is possessed of an accumulation of superior resources shall march against the enemy; whoever is devoid of power shall seek the protection of another; and whoever can accomplish his end by associating with an ally shall adopt a dual policy.

Peace is always to be preferred to war, for war brings loss of men, dissipation of wealth, and uncertainty of victory. The wise ruler exhausts diplomacy, alliance, and conciliation before unsheathing the sword.

## Book VIII: Concerning Vices and Calamities (Vyasanas)

A calamity (vyasana) is that which deprives a limb of its virtues and efficiency. The calamity of the king is more serious than that of the minister; the calamity of the minister is more serious than that of the country; the calamity of the country is more serious than that of the fort; that of the fort is more serious than that of the treasury; that of the treasury is more serious than that of the army; and that of the army is more serious than that of the ally.

For the king who is sovereign is the central hub. The king appoints the ministers; he attends to the works of the treasury; he constructs the forts and commands the forces. If the king is virtuous, he makes the other limbs virtuous; if he is vicious and negligent, he ruins the kingdom even though it is prosperous and secure.

Therefore, self-discipline and righteousness (Dharma) are the paramount armour of the sovereign. Protected by Dharma, the king protects his people; and protected by the people, his lineage flourishes through the ages.
